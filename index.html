While AI has touched every partner booth, panel discussion and pitch deck, the question underneath all of it is simpler than the pitches make it sound: does any of it actually change the file? Not: is the technology impressive? Not: does it reduce handling time? Not: is it the future? The question is simpler. At the claim level - on the specific file that sits on the desk - does it produce fewer supplements, fewer returns, less desk rework? Does the observational record it helps build hold up under review? AI handles some things well: triage, pattern recognition across large data sets, documentation review, flagging anomalies. These are real productivity gains for the operations that use them. AI doesn't handle other things: the judgment calls at inspection. The policyholder conversation that decides the file's trajectory. The observational record that has to hold up under review. The context that only comes from standing on the property. The technology isn't the answer carriers are looking for, the file is. #ClaimsWithAspen #AIinClaims #ClaimsOperations

The claims operation hiring problem nobody talks about.

What partner workforce strategy actually looks like - and why carriers should start asking about it.

Read the article draft
The Claims Operation Hiring Problem Nobody Talks About

The partner evaluation conversations I'm part of cover a lot of ground. Capacity. Pricing. Technology. Geographic coverage. SLA structure. File quality metrics. Over the last few years, the depth of these conversations has grown - carriers are asking harder questions, and the better partners have gotten better at answering them.

One topic almost never comes up. It's the one that quietly shapes everything else.

Workforce strategy.

How partners recruit, train, retain, and develop the adjusters doing the actual work on the carrier's files. The industry rarely talks about it directly, which means carriers rarely evaluate it directly. The result is that two partners can look identical on a scorecard while running operations that are structurally different in ways that affect every file.

Here's what the hiring problem actually looks like inside the IA industry, and why I think it should be a bigger part of partner evaluation than it is today.

The scale of the issue

Claims adjusting is a profession where the experienced practitioners are aging out faster than new entrants are coming in. The reasons are familiar to anyone inside the industry: the work is demanding, the path into the profession is less clear than it was twenty years ago, the compensation structure doesn't always compete with adjacent industries, and the training required to be genuinely good at the work takes years.

The result is a growing gap between the number of adjusters the industry needs and the number of adjusters the industry has - particularly adjusters with the depth of experience that complex and catastrophe work requires.

That gap shows up operationally in two different ways. Some partners are trying to close it by lowering the experience bar at hire and compressing training timelines. Other partners are investing more in longer development pipelines and higher-touch retention, accepting the operational cost of that approach because the alternative produces files that don't hold up.

From a carrier's perspective, the two approaches produce visibly different outcomes six to twelve months into a relationship. But at the point of partner selection, both approaches often look the same on the capabilities deck.

Why carriers don't ask about it

The honest answer is that workforce strategy is uncomfortable to evaluate from outside. Carriers can see the output - files, cycle time, communication quality - but the inputs that produce the output (who's hired, how they're trained, how long they stay) are internal to the partner's operation.

The questions required to evaluate workforce strategy also feel more invasive than typical RFP questions. Asking a partner about their adjuster turnover rate, their training investment per adjuster, their career progression structure, or their leadership tenure can feel like asking about the partner's internal business rather than the carrier's book.

So the questions don't get asked. Partner selection decisions get made on the metrics that are visible and comparable. Workforce strategy stays in the partner's internal conversation, not the carrier's.

The problem is that workforce strategy is one of the clearest predictors of what the next five years of a partner relationship will actually look like.

What a partner's workforce strategy actually affects

Three operational outcomes trace directly back to how a partner manages its workforce.

File quality consistency over time. Partners with high adjuster turnover deliver files whose quality varies by assignment, because the adjuster handling the file is changing. Partners with strong retention deliver files whose quality holds steady, because the same experienced adjusters return across deployments. Over a year of files, that difference compounds into materially different carrier experiences.

Depth available for complex and catastrophe work. Daily volume can be handled by less experienced adjusters with adequate training. Complex losses, large losses, and catastrophe deployments require depth that only comes from years of experience. Partners whose workforce strategy includes developing and retaining adjusters at that experience level have depth available when carriers need it. Partners who don't, don't - regardless of what the roster number says.

Relationship continuity across the carrier account. The strongest partner-carrier relationships are usually anchored by specific people - a desk adjuster who's been on the account for years, a field manager who understands the carrier's preferences, a leader who can be reached directly when something complicated comes up. Those people exist only if the partner's workforce strategy produces them. High-turnover operations can't offer this consistency, which is why carriers who work with those partners describe the relationships as transactional even when the files are fine.

Each of these outcomes shows up in the carrier's experience long after the initial partner selection decision was made. By the time the pattern is visible, the carrier is already deep into a contract.

What carriers should ask

If workforce strategy belongs in partner evaluation, four questions worth adding to the conversation.

The first - what does your adjuster retention look like over the last three years? Partners who track this can tell you. Partners who don't track it usually can't. The number itself matters less than whether the partner is paying attention to it as a managed outcome.

The second - how do you develop adjuster experience in-house versus hire it in? The strongest partners have both answers - they hire experienced adjusters where it's possible, and they have internal development pathways for the experience they can't buy. Partners who only answer one side of this are usually operating with a structural gap.

The third - what's the tenure of your leadership team? A partner whose leadership team has been together for years has institutional memory that affects every operational decision. A partner whose leadership rotates frequently has to rebuild context constantly, and that shows up in how the partner's operation evolves over time.

The fourth - what percentage of your current workforce has been with you for more than five years? This is the metric that most cleanly separates partners investing in retention from partners treating their workforce as replaceable. The two approaches produce different operations and different carrier experiences.

Where this leaves us

The hiring problem inside the IA industry isn't going to resolve itself in the next few years. The demographic trends are what they are, and the structural challenges in attracting new entrants to the profession are real.

What carriers can do is make workforce strategy a visible variable in partner evaluation. The partners who've made serious investments in building and keeping experienced adjusters benefit when that investment gets recognized. The partners who haven't should be visible as the ones who haven't.

Right now, that visibility doesn't exist in most partner conversations. Changing that is one of the simpler improvements the carrier-partner evaluation process could make, and it's probably one of the most consequential for how claims operations evolve over the next decade.

Worth adding to the conversation before the next contract cycle.

Twelve months after a claim closes, the policyholder remembers one thing.
Read the script
Twelve months after a claim closes, when the renewal notice shows up, the policyholder is making a decision. They're deciding whether to stay with the carrier or move on. And they're making that decision based on something very specific.

It isn't the settlement amount. They've forgotten the exact number. It isn't the cycle time. They don't remember how many days it took. It isn't the technology or the portal or the self-service tools.

It's how the claim felt.

Whether the adjuster sounded like a person or a process. Whether the explanations made sense or had to be chased. Whether promises got kept. Whether the moments when things got hard felt like someone was handling it, or like they were on their own.

That's the memory. That's what the renewal decision rests on.

This matters operationally because it changes what matters during the file. The documentation discipline we talk about has to be there - it decides whether the file closes clean. But the human discipline matters just as much. Returning calls when you said you would. Explaining things in language the policyholder can follow. Handling the hard conversations with care instead of defensiveness.

The policyholder won't remember any of it specifically. They'll remember how it felt. And twelve months later, that memory is going to be the renewal decision - not the premium.

Before every major CAT deployment, one question.
Read the script
Before every major CAT deployment, our leadership team has a readiness conversation. We check capacity. We check roster activation. We check field support, communication protocols, carrier commitments. The usual list.

But there's one question I always ask that isn't on the readiness checklist. It's the question that tells me whether we're actually ready, or just operationally ready.

The question is: what's going to break first?

Not what might break. Not what we're worried about. What is actually going to break first - because something always does. The question forces the team to be honest about where our weakest point is for this specific event, this specific geography, this specific intensity.

Sometimes the answer is a communication gap with a specific carrier whose desk team is new. Sometimes it's capacity in a sub-region we haven't deployed into recently. Sometimes it's a workflow we know is held together with discipline that's going to be hard to hold at surge volume.

Whatever the answer is, naming it before the event starts means we can prepare for it. Pretending it doesn't exist means we discover it in Week 2, when there's no time to fix it.

Every operation has a weakest point. The strong ones name it before the event. The others find out the hard way.
This one describes how your leadership team prepares, so it is a draft for you to make your own. It is not produced until your version comes back.
